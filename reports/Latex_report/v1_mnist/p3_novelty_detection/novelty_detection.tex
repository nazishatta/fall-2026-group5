\section{Overview and Scope}
Following the project's Phase 3 objective, we examine whether a frozen self-organizing map (SOM) and comparison detectors can recognize feature representations that differ from MNIST. A trained MNIST convolutional classifier provides 84-dimensional \texttt{fc2} embeddings, and a frozen $15\times15$ SOM supplies the reference prototypes. MNIST training embeddings ($n=49{,}000$) support fitting; MNIST validation ($n=10{,}500$) forms the in-distribution (ID) evaluation set. The proposal-specified SVHN test split ($n=26{,}032$) is the current external out-of-distribution (OOD) evaluation set, following the instructor clarification on 19 October 2026. Fashion-MNIST and KMNIST are deferred to later phases and are not part of the current Phase 3 results. These are development comparisons, not a final locked MNIST test result.

\section{Related Work and Project Contribution}
Kohonen introduced self-organizing maps, a topology-preserving organization of inputs into neuron prototypes \parencite{kohonen1990som}. Unlike work developing the SOM itself, this study applies an already-trained map as a frozen novelty reference. Vesanto and Alhoniemi investigated clustering of SOM representations \parencite{vesanto2000clustering}; our use of the U-Matrix focuses instead on local prototype separation as a candidate novelty signal.

LeCun and coauthors described convolutional approaches to document recognition \parencite{lecun1998gradient}. In this project, an MNIST CNN is not trained for OOD classification; its frozen intermediate embeddings provide the shared space for downstream SOM and distance-based detectors. The NNSOM library documentation from Jafari provides the implementation context for the project's SOM component \parencite{jafari_nnsom}; the contribution here is comparative post-training evaluation rather than a new SOM library.

Hendrycks and Gimpel established the maximum softmax probability (MSP) as a simple confidence-based reference for detecting misclassified and out-of-distribution inputs \parencite{hendrycks2017baseline}. Low maximum class probability can indicate uncertainty, but high confidence need not imply that an image resembles training data. We evaluate the frozen CNN's MSP alongside SOM quantization error (QE), which measures distance in embedding space rather than classification confidence.

Liu and colleagues proposed energy-based OOD detection using a scalar score computed from classifier logits \parencite{liu2020energy}. Energy uses the logit distribution rather than only the largest softmax probability and can offer a useful alternative OOD ranking. Our comparison evaluates the recorded energy score from the same frozen MNIST classifier without retraining a detector or claiming that QE is superior.

Lee and colleagues introduced a Mahalanobis-distance framework that models neural features through class-conditional statistics \parencite{lee2018mahalanobis}. Such distances detect departures from learned feature distributions but depend on their estimated means and covariance structure. Our study compares a training-fitted Mahalanobis reference against the SOM's nearest-prototype distance on the recorded MNIST representation.

Sun and colleagues investigated nearest-neighbor distances in deep representation space for OOD detection \parencite{sun2022knn}. Their approach assesses how close an example lies to reference training embeddings without requiring an explicit SOM grid. We use a recorded $k$NN-distance baseline in the same frozen feature space to test whether SOM topology offers benefits beyond ordinary neighbor proximity.

Liang and colleagues introduced ODIN, which modifies neural confidence scoring by applying temperature scaling and small input perturbations \parencite{liang2018odin}. These adjustments aim to sharpen separation between in-distribution and OOD inputs, but the perturbation procedure differs from evaluating an unchanged frozen embedding through SOM QE. We discuss ODIN as related work only; \emph{no ODIN benchmark was run for the results in this report}, so its performance is not inferred or inserted into the tables.

Isolation Forest uses random partitioning to isolate unusual observations \parencite{liu2008isolation}. Here we evaluate its novelty scores on the frozen CNN representations rather than raw features, and report its poor historical ranking without reversing the score after observing OOD labels. One-Class SVM estimates a support boundary for typical data \parencite{scholkopf2001support}; our application fits that boundary on MNIST embeddings, while noting that its historical fitting budget differs from other baselines.

Sakurada and Yairi examined reconstruction-based anomaly detection using autoencoders \parencite{sakurada2014autoencoder}. Our feature autoencoder similarly uses reconstruction error, but operates on frozen classifier embeddings, and its historical score was distorted by unscaled inputs (Section~\ref{sec:diagnostics}).

Nalisnick and colleagues showed that deep generative models trained on one image set can give higher likelihood to a different set; the best-known case is SVHN being rated as more typical than the CIFAR-10 images the model was trained on \parencite{nalisnick2019deep}. Our feature autoencoder and Isolation Forest show a similar inversion on SVHN, but in a different setting: they are fitted to a frozen classifier's embeddings rather than to pixels, and our diagnostics relate the inversion to the much shorter SVHN embeddings (Section~\ref{sec:diagnostics}).

\section{Methods and Frozen Experimental Setup}
\subsection{Representation, SOM and Quantization Error}
The SOM has 225 units and uses an 84-dimensional representation. For scaled embedding $z(x)$ and SOM weights $w_j$, the best matching unit and quantization error are
\begin{equation}
 b(x)=\arg\min_j \lVert z(x)-w_j\rVert_2,\qquad
 s_{\mathrm{QE}}(x)=\lVert z(x)-w_{b(x)}\rVert_2.
\end{equation}
Higher QE means a sample's embedding lies farther from its nearest map prototype. The historical decision threshold is the 95th percentile of MNIST training QE, $\tau=1.5690279077$; a sample with QE $>\tau$ is flagged. The 19 October 2026 instructor clarification confirmed retaining this training-percentile threshold from the original Phase 3 proposal. Earlier, conflicting validation-threshold review guidance and its resolution are recorded in \texttt{src/v1\_mnist/docs/DEVIATIONS.md}; the threshold was not retroactively tuned.

\subsection{U-Matrix and Comparison Methods}
For unit $j$ with grid neighbors $\mathcal N(j)$, a neighborhood-distance summary is
\begin{equation}
 U_j=\frac{1}{|\mathcal N(j)|}\sum_{k\in\mathcal N(j)}\lVert w_j-w_k\rVert_2.
\end{equation}
The example-level U-Matrix signal uses the score of an example's BMU neighborhood. It measures prototype-to-prototype separation, not necessarily distance of the example from its BMU. The original historical five-method SVHN comparison additionally includes Isolation Forest, One-Class SVM and feature autoencoder reconstruction error. Subsequent comparisons add MSP, energy, Mahalanobis and $k$-nearest-neighbor distance on the frozen CNN representation. Different historical fitting budgets remain documented limitations rather than being silently equalized.

\section{Quantitative Evaluation}
We report AUROC as a threshold-independent ranking measure. AUPR uses OOD as the positive class and depends on class prevalence. FPR@95TPR denotes the minimum observed ROC false-positive rate at an operating point with TPR at least $0.95$; it must not be confused with the false-positive rate at the previously frozen QE threshold \parencite{fawcett2006roc,saito2015pr}. We do not use observed external-OOD results to change historical model settings.

\subsection{Where SVHN lands on the map}\label{sec:overlay}
The proposal asks for novelty scores shown on the SOM grid. Run \run{mnist_svhn_som_overlay_s42_v1} assigns every SVHN test image to its best-matching unit with the frozen CNN and SOM; the recomputed QE values agree with the saved historical scores to within $10^{-6}$. The 26,032 SVHN images occupy only 28 of the 225 units, and a single unit (unit 79, whose MNIST training digits are mostly 4s, class purity 0.75) receives 22,521 of them (86.5\%). The 996 images missed at the frozen cutoff fall in 23 units, 355 of them in unit 79. Even the missed images lie farther from their prototype than ordinary digits in the same unit: their median QE is 1.23 times the median MNIST-validation QE of their unit, and only 7.6\% are at or below it.

These numbers describe where SVHN lands and where the detector fails; they do not explain why. They are also what the SOM adds beyond a single novelty score such as MSP: the map names the few regions where novel inputs collapse and the units that hold the misses.

\subsection{Unexpected baseline rankings and diagnostic status}\label{sec:diagnostics}
The historical Isolation Forest (AUROC $0.2383$) and feature autoencoder (AUROC $0.0077$) fall substantially below the chance-level value of $0.5$ under the recorded score definitions (Table~\ref{tab:svhn}). This means that the recorded scores rank many evaluated ID/OOD pairs opposite to the desired ``larger means more novel'' ordering; it does \emph{not} by itself establish a programming error. A separate validation-only reproduction confirmed seven historical MNIST score and loss summaries, but it did not recompute the SVHN AUROCs or recover the original autoencoder checkpoint.

Later diagnostics, each under its own run ID, narrowed the cause. The fc2 embeddings of SVHN are much shorter than those of MNIST: a length-only score (longer means more novel) has AUROC 0.00007 (95\% interval [0.00004, 0.00010]), with mean lengths of 40.3 for MNIST validation and 5.8 for SVHN (\run{mnist_svhn_autoencoder_scaling_diagnostic_s42_46_v1}). Inverting SVHN intensities changes the mean length only from 5.82 to 6.09 (\run{svhn_inverted_embedding_norm_s42_v1}), so light-on-dark polarity does not explain it. When the autoencoder's inputs are standardized with training statistics, its AUROC rises from about 0.01 to $0.979 \pm 0.017$ with 10,000 training rows and $0.902 \pm 0.072$ with 49,000 rows (five seeds); unstandardized inputs stay near 0.01 at both sizes. The autoencoder's inversion is therefore an input-scale effect: short SVHN embeddings are easy to reconstruct. Isolation Forest stays below chance even on standardized inputs (about 0.28), so its ranking remains unexplained. The pattern resembles the likelihood inversion reported for deep generative models on SVHN \parencite{nalisnick2019deep}.

Whether part of QE's own AUROC is a length effect is tested by \run{mnist_svhn_qe_norm_dependence_s42_v1}: the Spearman correlation of QE with embedding length is [TBD], and QE's AUROC among images whose length lies between the 5th and 95th percentiles of MNIST training lengths is [TBD]. We preserve the original AUROCs and do not flip signs, retune, or reinterpret the reported benchmark after inspecting the OOD outcomes.

\section{Leakage Control and Reproducibility}
The CNN and SOM were frozen for the documented Phase 3 comparisons. The additionally measured SVHN kNN baseline ($k=5$) and paired QE--MSP comparison are reproducible using the committed runner and fixed configuration; both reproduction and previous run IDs remain separate and immutable. Training embeddings supplied model fitting and the training-derived QE threshold. MNIST validation supplied ID evaluation examples; the official MNIST final-test split was not used for the reported Phase 3 results. External SVHN test samples supplied observed OOD evaluation evidence, not inputs for post-hoc fitting or threshold selection. Fashion-MNIST is outside this report's current evaluation scope. As these external sets have now been examined, further configuration changes using their outcomes would be exploratory and require a new protocol and run IDs; they must not be represented as independent validation.

The preregistration document's recorded writing date does not establish from Git history alone that its criteria were fixed before all external OOD results were examined. Historical JSON evidence is preserved under run-specific directories. The file \path{outputs/v1_mnist/p3_novelty_detection/run_manifest.csv} lists every remaining run folder with the script and config that wrote it and how that link was checked; where a result stores the SHA-256 of its config, the committed config still has that fingerprint, and a repository test enforces this. Repeat runs that only duplicated earlier numbers were removed; Git history keeps them, and \path{src/v1_mnist/docs/DEVIATIONS.md} records why. Each figure in this report is the PDF written by the run named in its caption; where that run recorded the figure's SHA-256, a test checks that the report copy matches it. SHA-256 input provenance and the run-ID overwrite preflight remain part of every step. The full test suite passed on EC2 for this version of the code: [TBD: number of tests].\par

A separate, newly identified verification run (\path{mnist_historical_baseline_verification_s42_v1}) reproduced all seven historical MNIST-validation summary measurements with zero absolute numerical difference: Isolation Forest and One-Class SVM score means and standard deviations, and the feature autoencoder initial loss, final loss and mean validation reconstruction error. Frozen training and validation embedding SHA-256 fingerprints matched the references. This verification used MNIST training/validation evidence only; its seven comparisons are summary statistics, not a rerun of the full SVHN discrimination experiment. This supports numerical agreement under the currently committed settings, not byte-identical regeneration of the original historical JSON, checkpoint, or execution environment. The original autoencoder checkpoint and historical package-version manifest remain unavailable; old metrics were not overwritten. No final MNIST test or external OOD samples were accessed during this validation-only reproduction.

\section{Limitations and Interpretation}
This report provides fixed-model bootstrap sampling intervals and a paired QE--MSP comparison, and a five-seed assessment of freshly fitted comparison methods, but not variability across five independent CNN/SOM retrainings. The original One-Class SVM used 10,000 MNIST training embeddings whereas some other historically fitted baselines used 49,000. In a separate post-hoc five-seed exploratory evaluation, five newly fitted methods shared 10,000 training examples per seed; four reference methods remained frozen, so not all nine underwent equivalent retraining. The cause of the historical autoencoder score behavior and the below-chance Isolation Forest ranking remains unresolved; orientation, consistent preprocessing, and positive-label definitions require a prospective diagnostic evaluation. The separate seven-summary reproduction does not validate the original SVHN AUROCs or establish an identical historic environment. No final locked MNIST test-set result is claimed. The comparison demonstrates useful novelty ranking on the examined distributions, not universal or clinically transferable OOD behavior.

\section{Conclusion and Next Phase}
The primary MNIST-validation versus SVHN comparison finds energy and MSP above SOM QE in AUROC, while QE remains a meaningful frozen-prototype distance signal. As a detector, the SOM therefore does not beat the classifier's own confidence. What it adds is location: the map shows SVHN collapsing onto 28 of 225 units, mostly one, and names the units that hold the images QE misses (Section~\ref{sec:overlay}), which the guided-retraining phase can target. The paired QE--MSP comparison confirms this difference for these fixed samples with a resampling interval below zero. The historical SVHN comparison shows stronger QE ranking relative to its original comparison set, but the additional SVHN baselines outperform it. A future prospectively specified study should extend the exploratory common-sample and multi-seed controls to fully matched fitting budgets and independently retrained CNN/SOM models, resolve historical reproduction gaps, and fix the final selection rules before using the untouched MNIST final-test split. These Phase 3 outputs can inform the project's subsequent guided-retraining work without prematurely claiming final benchmark success.
